# HVAC, ventilation and plumbing

## Plain-language summary
HVAC means heating, ventilation and air conditioning. NBC(AE) 2023 covers it in two places. Part 6 applies to the buildings listed in Division A 1.3.3.2, such as assembly, care and high-hazard buildings, and to residential, business, mercantile and industrial buildings over 600 m² or over three storeys [Sentence 1.3.3.2.(1), Div A, PDF pp.39-40]. Part 9 covers houses and other small buildings of listed occupancies, up to three storeys and 600 m² in building area [Sentence 1.3.3.3.(1), Div A, PDF p.40]. Part 9 ventilation is in 9.32, and heating and cooling are in 9.33 [Div B, Sentence 9.32.1.1.(1), PDF p.1040; Div B, Sentence 9.33.1.1.(1), PDF p.1055].

A Part 9 house needs a principal ventilation fan that runs to keep fresh air moving through the house [Sentence 9.32.3.3.(1), PDF p.1044]. It also needs kitchen and bathroom exhaust fans [Sentence 9.32.3.7.(1), PDF p.1048] and protection against depressurization, which is negative pressure that can pull combustion gases back down a flue [Sentence 9.32.3.8.(1), PDF p.1048]. The usual route is CSA F326 good practice, or the rest of 9.32.3 for dwellings with five or fewer bedrooms [Sentence 9.32.3.1.(1), PDF p.1043].

Plumbing has a separate code. Part 7 applies to every building [Sentence 1.3.3.1.(1), Div A, PDF p.39]. Plumbing systems must be designed and installed under the Plumbing Code Regulation made under the Safety Codes Act [Div B, Sentence 7.1.2.1.(1), PDF p.621]. The Act defines a plumbing system as drainage, venting and water systems [source p.10].

Other disciplines take over at defined points. Electrical installations go to the Electrical Code Regulation [Sentence 9.34.1.1.(1), PDF p.1064], and gas appliance installation goes to the gas code [Sentence 9.33.5.2.(1), PDF p.1057]. Each discipline has its own permit in the Permit Regulation (building s.6(1), electrical s.8(1), gas s.12(1), plumbing s.14(1)) [source pp.8, 11, 13, 14].

Two issues come up most. Outdoor exhaust clearances are changed by STANDATA 23-BCV-008(REV1) [source p.1]. Heat recovery ventilator (HRV) low-temperature performance is set in 9.32.3.10.(5) [Sentence 9.32.3.10.(5), PDF p.1050]. Carbon monoxide (CO) alarms are required where a fuel-burning appliance or storage garage is in a residential building [Sentence 9.32.3.9.(1), PDF p.1049].

## When this topic applies
Trigger signals. Any of these in a project description routes the question here.
- Heating and cooling equipment for one dwelling unit: furnace, boiler, air conditioner, heat pump, radiant heating in a house with a secondary suite [Sentence 9.33.1.1.(1), PDF p.1055].
- Ventilation: HRV or energy recovery ventilator, principal ventilation fan, kitchen, bathroom or dryer exhaust, makeup air, duct systems, outdoor intakes and exhausts [Sentence 9.32.3.3.(1), PDF p.1044; Sentence 9.32.3.13.(1), PDF p.1053].
- CO alarms wherever a fuel-burning appliance or storage garage sits in a residential building [Sentence 9.32.3.9.(1), PDF p.1049; Sentence 6.9.3.1.(1), PDF p.608].
- Plumbing: water supply, fixtures, drains, sewer connection, water heaters, backwater valves and backflow protection [Sentence 9.31.3.1.(1), PDF p.1039; Sentence 9.31.4.1.(1), PDF p.1039; Sentence 9.31.6.2.(1), PDF p.1040].
- Roof-mounted HVAC equipment and its access [Article 3.6.4.7., PDF p.294; Sentence 9.33.1.2.(1), PDF p.1055].
- Commercial cooking exhaust [Article 6.3.1.6., PDF p.597].
- Part 3 service rules for larger buildings: air duct materials and return air [Sentence 3.6.5.1.(1), PDF p.294; Article 3.6.5.8., PDF p.297], and plumbing facilities [Sentence 3.7.2.1.(5), PDF p.297]. These follow the same Division A 1.3.3.2 building list as Part 6.

Applicability tests. Decide Part 9 or Part 6 first.
- Part 9 applies to residential and small buildings within Division A 1.3.3.3(1) [Sentence 1.3.3.3.(1), Div A, PDF p.40].
- Part 6 applies to buildings within Division A 1.3.3.2(1) [Sentence 1.3.3.2.(1), Div A, PDF pp.39-40].
- Part 9 houses still reach Part 6 at specific points. Heating-season ventilation for a residential occupancy is sent to Part 6 under Div B, Clause 9.32.1.2.(1)(b), except where Sentences (2) and (3) apply [Sentence 9.32.1.2.(1), Div B, PDF p.1041]. A self-contained system serving a single dwelling unit follows 9.32.3 [Sentence 9.32.1.2.(2), Div B, PDF p.1041].
- Heating and air conditioning that do not serve only one dwelling unit go to Part 6, except radiant heating in a house with a secondary suite, which stays in Part 9 [Sentence 9.33.1.1.(1), Div B, PDF p.1055; Sentence 9.33.1.1.(2), Div B, PDF p.1055].
- Ventilation of all other occupancies complies with Part 6 [Sentence 9.32.1.1.(2), Div B, PDF p.1040].

Near misses that belong to another topic.
- Electrical work for fans, HRVs, heaters or thermostats, meaning wiring, circuits and receptacles. These belong to the electrical discipline [Sentence 9.34.1.1.(1), PDF p.1064; Permit Regulation s.8(1), source p.11]. The power connection rule for CO alarms stays here [Sentence 9.32.3.9.(2)(c), PDF p.1049].
- Gas piping, gas appliance installation and gas permits. These belong to the gas discipline [Permit Regulation s.12(1), source p.13]. Venting of combustion products stays here [Sentence 9.33.10.1.(1), PDF p.1064].
- Fire dampers and smoke detectors in duct systems. These belong to the fire-separations topic [Article 3.1.8.10., PDF p.155, referenced from Article 6.9.2.1.] and to the firefighting-alarms topic [Article 3.2.4.12., PDF p.222, referenced from Article 6.9.2.2.].
- Heat recovery efficiency and heat pump equipment performance. These belong to the energy-efficiency topic [Sentence 9.36.3.9.(3), PDF p.1083; Sentence 9.36.3.10.(1), PDF p.1084].
- Combustible duct materials in combustible or mass-timber buildings. These belong to the combustible-construction topic [Sentence 3.6.5.1.(2), PDF p.294].
- Duct systems that serve one unit of a house with a secondary suite. Interconnection is barred in Sentence 9.33.1.1.(3) [PDF p.1055], which belongs to the housing topic.
- Rainwater tanks and greywater reuse. The National Building Code text does not address them (UNVERIFIED for the plumbing code, see Open questions).

### Reading the attributions
The pairs are the attribution rows, not a list of everything the article touches. For example, 9.32.3.10.(5), the HRV cold-weather rule, is attributed to [F81-OH1.1,OH1.2], which is the functional statement to minimize the risk of malfunction and the objectives for indoor air quality and thermal comfort. A variance to that sentence therefore needs evidence on the same objectives.

## Intent
The NBC 2020 and NPC 2020 intent statements are explanatory supplements. They describe why an article exists. They are not Alberta law, and every intent citation below carries its supplement label. Each intent is written as a limit on the probability of a harm, so an alternative solution has to show that the same probability is still limited.

Heat recovery ventilator cold-weather performance. Sentence 9.32.3.10.(5) exists to limit the probability that a heat recovery ventilator falls well below its expected performance in cold weather, which would leave the house short of fresh air and moisture control NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.10.(5) [PDF p.3034]. A cold-weather variance must show that the unit still delivers the intended ventilation at the outdoor temperature it will meet.

HRV exhaust backdraft dampers. Sentence 9.32.3.13.(11) requires backdraft dampers on exhaust outlets, except outlets serving an HRV. The intent gives the reason: the internal resistance of the HRV core is treated as enough to resist backdraft NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.13.(11) [PDF p.3050].

Outdoor clearances. Sentence 9.32.3.13.(3) limits the probability that exhaust from gas vents and oil fill pipes is drawn into a ventilation intake NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.13.(3) [PDF p.3046]. Sentence 9.32.3.13.(4) limits the probability that moist exhaust condensation enters the attic space and damages building elements NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.13.(4) [PDF p.3047]. The attic-moisture rationale in STANDATA 23-BCV-008(REV1) is aimed at that second intent [source p.2].

Carbon monoxide alarms. Sentence 9.32.3.9.(2) limits the probability that CO alarming devices fall below expectations, which would let CO reach a living space unnoticed. NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.9.(2) [PDF p.3030]. Sentence 9.32.3.9.(4) adds that the alarm must be loud enough to wake sleeping occupants. NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.9.(4) [PDF p.3030].

Good engineering practice. Sentence 6.2.1.1.(1) limits the probability that heating, ventilating or air-conditioning systems perform well below expectations because of poor design, construction or operation. The intent names the chain of harm: fire, toxic gas release or unsafe surface temperatures, and indoor conditions that cannot be kept comfortable or dry. NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 6.2.1.1.(1) [PDF p.1061].

Backwater valves (plumbing, NPC supplement). The NPC 2020 explanatory supplement, in its Protection from Backflow entry, says the backflow rule limits the probability that a backup of public sewers backflows into building drains where fixtures are below the level of the adjoining street. NPC 2020 explanatory supplement, Protection from Backflow, third sentence [source p.110]. The supplement text that follows the fourth sentence says its purpose is to modify the third sentence and allow a backwater valve on the branch. NPC 2020 explanatory supplement, Protection from Backflow, fourth sentence [source p.111]. The extraction does not print the objective and attribution lines for the fourth sentence, so that attachment is by position. That is the intent behind the Calgary advisory discussed under Precedents. The NPC text itself is not in the reference set, so the wording of the allowance is UNVERIFIED. The codedb gate covers NBC(AE) 2023 only, so these NPC citations were checked by reading source pages 110 and 111.

Intent comparison. For Sentence 9.32.3.9.(2), codedb's intent comparison reports that the national intent pairs match the Alberta pairs (status "match"). The national supplement is therefore a reliable explanation for that sentence, but it is still explanatory only.

## Typical alternative solutions
These are the proposals innovators actually bring, with the precedent behind each where one exists. A precedent covers only its own scope, and several were issued under earlier editions. Nothing here is a general permission.

Clearance reduction for moist-air exhaust near air intakes. STANDATA 23-BCV-008(REV1) lets kitchen, bathroom and clothes dryer exhaust outlets serving one dwelling unit, or a house with a secondary suite, sit 900 mm from an air intake where the outlet is level with the intake or above it. Air intakes include those connected to an HRV, an ERV or a furnace return plenum [source p.1]. Below the intake, the 1 800 mm clearance still applies [source p.3, Figure A]. The rationale is warm moist air entering the attic through vented soffits [source p.2]. The June 2024 original, 23-BCV-008, is marked archived on every page [source p.1 of that document].

Refrigerant standard edition. STANDATA 23-BCV-009 accepts CSA B52:23, Mechanical refrigeration code, as an installation standard under Sentences 6.2.1.5.(1) and 9.33.5.2.(1) [source p.1]. The STANDATA says the code then referenced the 2018 edition in Table 1.3.1.2 [source p.1]. The 2nd printing's Table 1.3.1.2 lists CSA B52:23 [Div B, Table 1.3.1.2., PDF p.90]. The CSA B52:23 row sits on a later page of that table, which codesearch confirmed. Whether the variance is still needed is an open question below.

Newer ventilation standard edition with engineer-of-record conditions. Calgary SPV-004 (2020) accepted a newer edition of the ASHRAE 62 ventilation standard in place of ANSI/ASHRAE 62-2001 [source p.1]. It required the engineer of record to sign and seal the mechanical drawings, name the edition in writing with a statement that the rates suit the occupancy, apply that edition to the whole project, and cite SPV-004 in the permit documents and conditions [source p.2]. It was written against the superseded NBC(AE) 2019 (historical context only) and needs re-checking against the 2nd printing before reuse.

Normally open backwater valve on a shared branch. Calgary Advisory Bulletin P15-002 (2022) says a normally open backwater valve may serve a building drain or branch if it does not serve more than one dwelling unit, and that a secondary suite is a separate dwelling unit under the NPC definition. A valve serving more than one unit may need to be removed or reconfigured [source p.1]. The bulletin says it has no legal status [source p.1]. The NPC explanatory supplement's intent for the sentence that allows a backwater valve on the branch is in the Intent section above.

Ground-source and air-source heat pumps. Ground-source systems are installed under CSA C448 [Div B, Sentence 9.33.5.2.(1), PDF p.1057]. Air-cooled air conditioners and heat pumps are rated on SEER2 and HSPF2, and STANDATA 23-BCB-006 explains those metrics against the minimum performance requirements of the NBC(AE) and the NECB [source p.1]. No variance precedent for heat pump performance was found.

Hydronic and radiant heating. Heating systems must follow good practice [Div B, Sentence 9.33.4.1.(1), PDF p.1056]. STANDATA 23-BCI-042 (July 2025) addresses the design and verification of hydronic heating systems, because safety codes officers asked for a process to verify good practice [source p.1]. Radiant heating in a house with a secondary suite stays in Part 9 [Div B, Sentence 9.33.1.1.(1), PDF p.1055].

Make-up air and appliance category. STANDATA 23-BCI-020 (March 2026) clarifies which appliances count as mechanically vented or direct-vented, and so when make-up air under the depressurization article is required [source p.1]. An applicant who proposes a direct-vent or mechanically vented appliance to avoid make-up air should cite this interpretation together with the appliance category.

Roof-mounted HVAC access. STANDATA 23-BCB-008/25-GCB-002 (June 2026) gives guidance on access to roof-mounted HVAC equipment under Article 3.6.4.7, read with CSA B149.1:25. It notes that the superseded 1st printing (historical context only) references CSA B149.1:20 [source p.1]. Current references should be cited.

HRV cold-weather performance below the reduction factor. No precedent was found in the local index. A variance would need a CAN/CSA-C439 low-temperature test at the design temperature, not colder than -25°C, plus evidence that the ventilation rate still holds. The worked example below works one such case.

Smoke detectors on smoke and fire-smoke dampers. STANDATA 23-BCI-008 (October 2024) states that the damper-closing smoke detector requirement in Sentence 3.1.8.11.(3) does not by itself require a building fire alarm system [source p.1]. This is a routing point for the fire topics, not a ventilation variance.

## Usual mitigations and evidence
Reviewers expect the mitigations below. Each is a requirement the code names, so an alternative that drops one must replace it with evidence on the same objective.

Performance evidence for ventilation. The code calls CAN/CSA-F326-M good practice for heating-season ventilation [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.1.(1), PDF p.1043]. The outdoor air flow is measured and set within ±10% of the principal fan exhaust, and the measurement method must be accurate to ±15% [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.4.(10), PDF p.1046; Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.4.(11), PDF p.1046]. The separate-fan route has the same tolerances [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.5.(6), PDF p.1047; Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.5.(7), PDF p.1047].

Fan and HRV ratings. Fan capacity is rated under CAN/CSA-C260-M or HVI 916 [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.10.(1), PDF p.1050]. Fan sound is rated under CAN/CSA-C260-M or HVI 915 [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.10.(2), PDF p.1050]. HRV airflow and the low-temperature ventilation reduction factor are rated under CAN/CSA-C439 [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.10.(4), PDF p.1050; Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.10.(5), PDF p.1050]. Mechanical ventilation devices conform to CSA C22.2 No. 113 [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.10.(7), PDF p.1050].

CO alarms. The devices conform to CSA 6.19, Residential carbon monoxide alarming devices [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.9.(2), PDF p.1049]. Part 6 buildings name the same device standard [Div B, Sentence 6.9.3.1.(2), PDF p.608].

Depressurization. A CAN/CGSB-51.71 depressurization test can replace make-up air where the appliance limits are met [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.8.(7), PDF p.1049].

Heating and installation. Heating capacity is sized under CSA F280 [Div B, Sentence 9.33.5.1.(1), PDF p.1057]. Installation follows CSA B52, CSA B139 and CSA C448 for ground-source heat pumps [Div B, Sentence 9.33.5.2.(1), PDF p.1057]. Equipment follows the manufacturer's instructions unless they conflict with 9.32.3 [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.2.(2), PDF p.1043]. HRV start-up follows the manufacturer's procedure, including air balancing and airflow measurement [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.12.(4), PDF p.1053].

Part 6 standards. Ventilation rates follow the ASHRAE 62.1 sections named in Sentence 6.3.1.1.(2) [Div B, Sentence 6.3.1.1.(2), PDF p.595]. Commercial cooking ventilation follows NFPA 96 [Div B, Article 6.3.1.6., PDF p.597]. Duct materials follow CAN/ULC-S110 for Class 1 duct materials [Div B, Sentence 3.6.5.1.(2), PDF p.294].

Test standards by number. The standards the code itself names are the ones a reviewer will look for. CAN/CSA-F326-M (ventilation design). CAN/CSA-C439 (HRV rating, including the low-temperature test at up to −25°C). CAN/CSA-C260-M and HVI 915 or 916 (fan rating and sound). CSA 6.19 (CO alarms). CAN/CGSB-51.71 (depressurization). CSA F280 (heating capacity). CSA B52, CSA B139 and CSA B365 (installation). CSA C448 (ground-source heat pumps). CSA B149.1 (roof access, cited through Article 3.6.4.7). ASHRAE 62.1 (Part 6 ventilation rates). Each is named in the article text itself, not only in a note or in Table 1.3.1.2, and each is cited to the provision above.

Evidence ladder. The confidence ladder, from manufacturer literature through STANDATA to accredited test reports and certification, belongs to the evidence topic. For this topic, a manufacturer's rating under C439 or HVI is the minimum evidence the code asks for, and an independent test to the same standard is stronger.

## Engineering / professional triggers
Registered professional triggers in Division C. Some small buildings do not need registered professional involvement. Division C 2.4.1.1.(1) lists the exempt buildings, including a single-family dwelling and a multiple-family dwelling with four or fewer dwelling units [Div C, Sentence 2.4.1.1.(1), PDF pp.739-740]. For that purpose a secondary suite is not counted as a dwelling unit [Div C, Sentence 2.4.1.1.(2), PDF pp.739-740]. For any other building, drawings and specifications must be authenticated by one or more registered professionals qualified in the relevant parts of architecture and engineering [Div C, Sentence 2.4.1.1.(3), PDF p.740]. The authority having jurisdiction can require registered professionals where the size or complexity of a project raises special safety concerns [Div C, Sentence 2.4.1.1.(4), PDF p.740]. Authentication follows the Architects Act or the Engineering and Geoscience Professions Act [Div C, Sentence 2.4.1.1.(5), PDF p.740].

Coordinating professional. A project within Sentence 2.4.1.1.(3) carries the coordinating-professional duty [Div C, Article 2.4.2.1., PDF p.741]. Before applying for a permit, the owner must retain a coordinating registered professional to coordinate the design and field reviews [Div C, Sentence 2.4.2.2.(1), PDF p.741].

Sealed drawings as a condition. The only HVAC precedent in the local index that requires a professional seal is Calgary SPV-004. It requires the engineer of record to sign and seal the mechanical drawings when a newer ventilation edition is used [source p.2].

Statutory power to require seals. The Safety Codes Act lets the regulations require designs to be signed or stamped by persons licensed under the Architects Act or the Engineering and Geoscience Professions Act [source p.53, s.65(b)]. A person who affixes a stamp must ensure the service complies with the Act [source p.14, s.11]. No regulation applying that power to HVAC or plumbing design was located in the reference set (UNVERIFIED).

What "practice of engineering" means in Alberta is defined in the Engineering and Geoscience Professions Act, which is not in the reference set (UNVERIFIED). The tool should not decide whether a given HVAC calculation is engineering. It should name the professional and stop.

## Worked example
This case is illustrative, not a real project. A builder designs a three-bedroom single-family house with no secondary suite. The principal ventilation fan is an HRV. The CSA C439 test at −25°C shows a low-temperature ventilation reduction factor of 45%, below the 50% the code asks for.

Step 1, route. The house is within Division A 1.3.3.3(1), so Part 9 applies [Div A, Sentence 1.3.3.3.(1), PDF p.40]. Heating-season ventilation can use the balance of 9.32.3 because the house has five or fewer bedrooms [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.1.(1), PDF p.1043]. The exhaust side of an HRV can satisfy the principal ventilation fan requirement [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.3.(3), PDF p.1044].

Step 2, the departure. The unit must reach a low-temperature ventilation reduction factor of at least 50%, tested under CSA C439 at the outdoor design temperature, but that temperature need not be colder than −25°C [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.10.(5), PDF p.1050]. The outdoor design temperature comes from the authority's values, or from Appendix C at the January 2.5% value [Div B, Sentence 1.1.3.1.(1), PDF p.89; Div B, Sentence 1.1.3.1.(3), PDF p.89]. The 45% result is the departure, so the proposal is an alternative solution to that sentence.

Step 3, what the variance must show. The attributions for the sentence are [F81-OH1.1,OH1.2] (Div B Table 9.38.1.1., PDF p.1220), so the alternative must show the same protection against malfunction and against the inadequate air quality and thermal comfort that those objectives describe. The national intent for the sentence is to limit the probability that HRV performance falls significantly below expectations in cold weather. NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.10.(5) [PDF p.3034].

Section C table for the alternative. The rows are the attributed articles that the departure touches.

| Article and provision | Applicable code or standard | Objective, function and attribution | Intent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.10.(5), PDF p.1050 | CAN/CSA-C439 low-temperature test | Objectives OH1.1 and OH1.2 with function F81: [F81-OH1.1,OH1.2] (Div B Table 9.38.1.1., PDF p.1220) | NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.10.(5) [PDF p.3034] |
| Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.12.(4), PDF p.1053 | Manufacturer start-up, air balancing and airflow determination | Pairs [F53-OH1.1,OH1.2], [F53,F63-OS2.3] and [F43,F53-OS3.4] (Div B Table 9.38.1.1., PDF p.1220) | NBC 2020 Intent Statements, 9.32.3.12.(4) [PDF p.3043] |

Step 4, the evidence a reviewer will look for. The checklist below lists what the evidence must contain, not whether it passes.
- A CAN/CSA-C439 low-temperature test report for the exact HRV model and core, showing the reduction factor and the test temperature used [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.10.(5), PDF p.1050].
- A calculation, by the designer, that the principal ventilation rate in Table 9.32.3.3 holds at the cold-weather factor measured [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.3.(2), PDF p.1044].
- A start-up and balancing record, completed as the manufacturer directs [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.12.(4), PDF p.1053].
- An energy check. If the HRV is the principal exhaust with heat recovery, Sentence 9.36.3.9.(1) applies [Div B, Sentence 9.36.3.9.(1), PDF p.1083]. For a location whose January 2.5% design temperature is below −10°C, Sentence 9.36.3.9.(3) also requires a sensible efficiency of at least 55% at −25°C [Div B, Sentence 9.36.3.9.(3), PDF pp.1083-1084]. This is a separate requirement, not a way around the cold-weather factor.
- A signed or sealed drawing where the authority requires registered professional involvement [Div C, Sentence 2.4.1.1.(4), PDF p.740].

Step 5, the out-of-scope electrical question. Suppose the applicant asks whether the HRV needs its own 15 A circuit, and what wire size and disconnect it needs. The tool does not answer that. Electrical installations, including service capacity and the number and distribution of circuits, conform to the Electrical Code Regulation [Div B, Sentence 9.34.1.1.(1), PDF p.1064]. Electrical systems need an electrical permit [source p.11, Permit Regulation s.8(1)], and the permit holder is listed in s.9(1), for example a master electrician [source p.12]. The tool should say: "Circuit and wire sizing belongs to the electrical discipline. Confirm it with a licensed electrical contractor and the electrical permit." The one electrical rule this topic does state is for CO alarms: a CO alarm powered by the dwelling's electrical system may not have a disconnect switch between the overcurrent device and the alarm [Div B, Sentence 9.32.3.9.(2)(c), PDF p.1049].

What the tool does not decide. It does not decide whether the alternative gives equal or better performance, whether the cold-weather factor is acceptable at a given site, or whether the design needs an engineer's seal. It collects the Section C rows and the evidence list, names the professional or discipline, and stops. Section D, the supporting documentation, is left to the professional, because the challenge scope does not expect the tool to complete it on its own [Challenge-Scope, source p.3].

## Open questions / uncertainty
- Alberta plumbing edition. The code that governs plumbing is the Plumbing Code Regulation, which NBC(AE) names in Sentence 7.1.2.1.(1) [Div B, Sentence 7.1.2.1.(1), PDF p.621]. The Safety Codes Act regulation list shows "Plumbing Code" as AR 119/2007 with later amendments [source p.4]. The regulation text is not in the reference set, so the Alberta plumbing rules are not quoted here. The alberta.ca page says the code in force is the National Plumbing Code of Canada 2020, in force April 1, 2023, and does not say whether that is an Alberta edition or give the AR number (WebFetch, accessed 2026-10-19). A trade article reports that Alberta will adopt NPC 2025 from 1 January 2027 (UNVERIFIED: the snippet was not checked against a government source).
- Reuse water. The NBC(AE) text has no greywater or rainwater reuse rule. Keyword search found only roof rain loads and a drain water heat recovery standard (CSA B55.1) among the referenced standards. The NPC explanatory supplement mentions rainwater tanks in a backflow context [source p.98]. The reuse rules themselves are UNVERIFIED and sit with the plumbing regulation.
- Bulletin 23-BCV-009 and the 2nd printing. STANDATA 23-BCV-009 (October 2024) says the code then referenced CSA B52:18 [source p.1]. The 2nd printing's Table 1.3.1.2 lists CSA B52:23 [Div B, Table 1.3.1.2., PDF p.90], and the row sits on a later page of that table. Whether the variance is still needed for the 2nd printing is UNVERIFIED. The precedent index also maps 23-BCV-009 to Article 6.3.1.4. The STANDATA text does not cite 6.3.1.4 except inside its quote of Sentence 6.2.1.5.(1), so the index mapping was too broad and was removed on 2026-10-19 (see research/precedents/errata.md).
- Calgary SPV-004 under the 2023 text. SPV-004 names ANSI/ASHRAE 62-2001 as referenced by the code [source p.1]. In the 2nd printing, the referenced standards table lists ANSI/ASHRAE 62-2001 with a note reference, A-9.25.5.2, and also lists ANSI/ASHRAE 62.1-2016. Part 6 cites ASHRAE 62.1 sections [Div B, Sentence 6.3.1.1.(2), PDF p.595]. Whether SPV-004 still has effect is UNVERIFIED.

The SPV-004 objective wording ("unsanitary conditions") is the OH2 wording in the 2023 text, while OH1 is "indoor conditions" [Div A objectives, PDF p.70].
- Part 6 and Part 9 cross-reference. Div B, Clause 9.32.1.2.(1)(b) sends heating-season ventilation for a residential occupancy to Part 6, but Division A 1.3.3.2 does not list Part 9 houses [Div A, Sentence 1.3.3.2.(1), PDF pp.39-40]. This sheet follows the cross-reference as written. The reconciliation is not verified against any Alberta guidance.
- Figure A. The clearance geometry was read from the rendered STANDATA page 3, not from the text layer. The 900 mm sides and top and the 1 800 mm bottom dimensions are verified. Whether Figure A applies to every air intake type, including an HRV or furnace return plenum, rests on the variance's own wording [source p.1].
- Heat pumps. This sheet names CSA C448 and the SEER2 and HSPF2 bulletin. The equipment performance values in Div B, Table 9.36.3.10. were not extracted, and the heat pump articles in Div B, Section 9.36. were not checked.
- Gas, electrical and private sewage regulations. The Gas Code Regulation, Electrical Code Regulation and Private Sewage Disposal Systems Regulation are cited by name only. Their text is not in the reference set.
- Named technical standards. CSA B52, B139, B365, C448, C439, C260, HVI 915 and 916, CSA 6.19, CAN/CGSB-51.71, CSA F280, CAN/CSA-F326-M, CSA B149.1 and CSA C22.2 No. 113 are named by the code. This sheet cites where the code names them and does not restate their requirements. Each would need its own verification before a fact about its content is used.
- Definitions pages. The 41 unbound-page warnings from the validator are the code-key definition pages, which codedb keeps in its definitions table rather than as provision nodes. No errors remain.
- Not checked. Division B Appendix C values; Division A 1.2.1.1 (alternative solution wording); the NRC alternative solution papers; the Edmonton emergency services and policy guides; and the full SCC variance form field list, which belongs to the process topic.
